Rethinking Long-Video Efficiency: A Joint Allocation Perspective on Frames, Pixels, and Front-End Latency

We are building longvideo-eval, an open-source framework for evaluating common long-video VLMs, and LoHi will be released as part of it.

Fewer frames. Less latency. Higher accuracy.

Efficient long-video VLMs usually ask which tokens to keep at native resolution. LoHi asks how to spend a fixed budget across frame count, per-frame resolution, and front-end decoding, and lands on an operating point no fixed-resolution method can reach.

Three lessons

What actually limits long-video VLMs

Every VLM spends a fixed visual-token budget B on one configuration (N, r): N frames at per-frame resolution scale r, with N · τ(r) ≤ B. Before the tokens ever reach the model, a video front-end must decode those frames from the compressed file. Play with each lesson below.

L1

Dense low-resolution sampling is the new recipe

At the same token budget, 256 frames at ¼ resolution beat 16 frames at native resolution, and beat dedicated keyframe-selection and token-pruning methods too. We call this Low-Res-Base.

L2

    L3

    Front-end latency cannot be overlooked in long videos

    A video file is not a stack of pictures. Codecs store a full I-frame at the start of each Group of Pictures (GoP), and every following P-frame only stores what changed since the previous frame. To show any P-frame, the decoder must start at its I-frame and replay every frame in between.

    Click any frame: how much must be decoded to get it? I-frame (self-contained)P-frame (stores changes)decoded

    Measured decode latency (CPU decord)

    Dense sampling touches every GoP and grows with video length. Uniform sampling of a fixed N caps both.

    Why it matters

    Decode-then-select keyframe methods (AKS, BOLT, CLIP top-K) first build a candidate pool by densely decoding the video, then score it. On a one-hour clip the decoder alone runs for tens of seconds while the GPU sits idle. Token pruning decodes 256 native-resolution frames and runs the full vision encoder before discarding 93.75% of tokens.

    LoHi bounds the pool: it decodes exactly N = 128 frames once, regardless of length, and reuses them at two resolutions. LoHi-Anchor even reads I-frame positions as a free scene-change hint, without decoding extra pixels.

    Takeaway (L3). Under dense candidate-pool decoding such as FPS=1, front-end latency grows with video length and eventually dominates end-to-end runtime. Long-video efficiency methods must therefore bound the size of the decoded candidate pool, not just the visual-token budget passed to the VLM.
    LoHi vs. prior work

    Keyframe selection, token pruning, or just resize?

    Prior efficient long-video methods keep frames at native resolution and decide what to throw away. Both families pay for it before the VLM sees a single token. Resizing is the option they skip, and it turns out to be the strongest.

    Method

    LoHi: low-resolution video + high-resolution images

    LoHi splits one configuration (N, r) into two complementary streams that share one budget and one decoding pass, and routes them through the VLM's own video and image pathways. Training-free, single forward pass, no architectural change.

    Cross-resolution decomposition

    N·τ(rℓ) + K·τ(rh) ≤ B,    Ndec = N

    A dense Lo-V stream of N frames at a low scale rℓ provides temporal coverage (L1). A sparse Hi-I stream of K ≪ N frames at rh supplies spatial detail (L2). The K Hi-I indices are a subset of the Lo-V grid, so each frame is decoded once at native resolution and reused at both scales: decode count depends only on N, not on video length (L3). Default: N = 128 at rℓ = 0.25, K = 8 at rh = 1.0.

    Two native pathways, one text bridge

    Lo-V goes through the video pathway, whose 3D mRoPE gives every frame a consistent temporal position so motion keeps its natural rate. Hi-I goes through the image pathway, where each image gets its own 2D positions and the video timeline stays undisturbed. A natural-language marker tells the VLM how the two relate:

    “[Video] The above low-resolution video provides temporal context. The following K high-resolution images show selected key frames: [img1], …, [imgK].”

    Plug-and-play Hi-I selectors

    LoHi-Uniform

    selection cost: zero

    Picks the K Hi-I frames at regular intervals directly from the Lo-V grid. Pure indexing, and the fundamental baseline of the framework.

    LoHi-Anchor

    selection cost: free codec metadata

    Encoders start GoPs with I-frames, which can align with scene changes or strong motion. For each uniform position, Anchor finds the nearest I-frame from pre-computed indices and selects the Lo-V grid frame closest to it, with no additional pixel decoding.

    LoHi-SemDiv

    selection cost: one CLIP pass on 128 small frames

    A quality–similarity DPP over CLIP embeddings: L = diag(q) EE⊤ diag(q), with q the sharpened query relevance. Greedy maximization of log det(LS + I) picks frames that are both query-relevant and mutually diverse, with a (1 − 1/e) guarantee.

    Adaptive LoHi. Because the streams are decoupled, LoHi can answer from the Lo-V base first and trigger Hi-I only when the answer entropy exceeds a threshold. This nearly matches the full pipeline while invoking the high-resolution stream on only one-third of the queries.

    Robustness

    A missed frame costs little, by design

    A sparse selector can miss the decisive frame. In LoHi that frame is never absent: it is still in the Lo-V stream at low resolution, which covers every sampled timestamp. Selection only affects the resolution-sensitive residual, never temporal coverage.

    Bounded worst case

    If every Hi-I frame were irrelevant, LoHi degrades to its Lo-V base: 63.00% on VideoMME. LoHi-SemDiv reaches 66.81%. Selection quality governs at most a 3.8-point residual on top of a floor guaranteed by coverage alone.

    Selector-agnostic gains

    Four training-free selectors × K ∈ {2, 4, 8}: all 12 configurations land in 65.22–66.81%, and even the weakest is +2.2 points above the Lo-V base. The gain comes from the two-stream design, not a lucky selector.

    Both streams are needed

    At 5,760 tokens, Lo-V alone (256 frames) gives 64.44% and Hi-I alone (16 SemDiv images) 63.74%. Splitting the budget into 128 Lo-V frames and 8 Hi-I images gives 66.81%.

    FAQ

    Questions we often get

    “Coverage vs. detail” sounds intuitive. What is new?

    The intuition is natural; it is how people skim a long video. What is new is that existing efficient methods do not act on it. Keyframe selection and token pruning work at fixed native resolution, none of them trades resolution for coverage, and none accounts for front-end decoding, which can exceed VLM inference on hour-scale clips.

    Framing efficiency as a joint allocation over frame count, per-frame resolution, and front-end latency immediately produces Low-Res-Base, a baseline prior work does not report, which outperforms every keyframe-selection and token-pruning baseline on average at matched budget. The same view is what makes LoHi's operating point visible: decode 128 instead of 256 frames, at about 65% of the dense baseline's TTFT, while scoring higher.

    What if the selector picks the wrong frames?

    The missed frame is still in the Lo-V stream at low resolution, so a miss is graceful. Under total selector failure LoHi falls back to its Lo-V base (63.00% on VideoMME), and in practice all 12 selector and K combinations stay within 65.22–66.81%. Failure cases and their remedies are visualized in the paper: Anchor recovers evidence when a uniform pick lands on an uninformative frame, and SemDiv retrieves transient or fine-detail evidence that falls between uniform picks.

    Why not just prune tokens?

    At the same number of tokens reaching the LLM, a plain resize matches or beats VisionZip, MMTok and FlashVid at both 25% and 50% budgets. Pruning still pays for native-resolution encoding (2,486 ms of ViT time vs. 195 ms for LoHi) and fragments the spatiotemporal structure that video models rely on.

    Where does the latency saving come from?

    Three places. LoHi decodes 128 frames instead of 256 (3.3 s vs. 5.4 s of CPU decoding on VideoMME). It never encodes native-resolution frames except the K Hi-I images. And its selectors are nearly free: Uniform and Anchor are indexing over the grid and codec metadata, and SemDiv runs one CLIP pass over 128 small frames.

    Does LoHi need training or model changes?

    No. It uses the video and image pathways that unified VLMs already expose, plus one natural-language marker. It transfers without retuning to Qwen3-VL-4B/8B, Qwen3.5-4B, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, VideoChat3-4B and VideoLLaMA3-7B.

    When will the code be released?

    We are building longvideo-eval, an open-source framework for evaluating common long-video VLMs, and LoHi will be released as part of it.

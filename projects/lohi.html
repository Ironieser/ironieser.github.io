Abstract

Efficient long-video understanding with vision-language models (VLMs) has largely been treated as informative token selection at fixed native resolution: which frames or which visual tokens to retain under a token budget. We argue this framing leaves two axes unused: per-frame resolution itself can be traded for denser temporal coverage, and front-end decoding latency scales with the candidate pool, not the token budget. Through an empirical study across multiple VLMs and long-video benchmarks, we distill three lessons: (i) dense low-resolution sampling outperforms sparse native-resolution sampling at matched token budgets; (ii) some tasks are resolution-sensitive and benefit from high-resolution frames; and (iii) front-end decoding dominates wall time on hour-scale clips. These lessons motivate LoHi, a training-free, single-pass framework that pairs a dense low-resolution video stream with a sparse set of high-resolution image streams, processed through the VLM's native video and image pathways. On three long-video benchmarks, LoHi improves over the vanilla native-resolution baseline by +10.6% on average at matched token budget and over the strongest prior efficiency methods by +5.2%, while reducing front-end decoding latency by up to 7× on hour-scale clips.

Three Lessons on Long-Video Understanding

The question is not which informative tokens to keep, but how to jointly allocate an end-to-end budget across frame count, per-frame resolution, and front-end latency

L1 · Dense low-resolution sampling is the new recipe

At a fixed token budget, trading per-frame resolution for denser temporal coverage consistently outperforms the sparse native-resolution recipe on long-video benchmarks.

L2 · Resolution sensitivity is task-dependent

Moderate downscaling preserves most accuracy, but aggressive downscaling fails on resolution-sensitive tasks such as OCR and attribute perception. The remaining deficit calls for a small amount of high-resolution evidence.

L3 · Front-end latency dominates on long videos

Decode-then-select pipelines build their candidate pool by densely decoding the video, so latency scales linearly with duration. On hour-scale videos it dominates wall time, while uniform fixed-budget sampling stays constant.

The LoHi Framework

Low-resolution video for temporal coverage, high-resolution images for spatial detail, one forward pass

Overview of LoHi. Compared with previous work (A), which prunes or selects within a native-resolution pathway, LoHi (B) decomposes one decoded video into two complementary streams: dense low-resolution Lo-V via the video pathway and sparse high-resolution Hi-I via the image pathway. (C) shows the LoHi-SemDiv selector, which picks Hi-I indices via greedy MAP.

Cross-resolution decomposition

N · τ(rℓ) + K · τ(rh) ≤ B   (token budget),    Ndec = N   (decode budget)

  • ✓ Lo-V: a dense stream of N frames at a low scale rℓ, routed through the video pathway, whose 3D mRoPE keeps motion at its natural rate.
  • ✓ Hi-I: K ≪ N frames at a higher scale rh, routed through the image pathway with independent 2D positions, leaving the video timeline undisturbed.
  • ✓ Decode once: the K Hi-I indices are a subset of the Lo-V grid, so each frame is decoded once at native resolution and reused at both scales. Decode count depends only on N, not on video length.
  • ✓ Natural-language bridge: “The above low-resolution video provides temporal context. The following K high-resolution images show selected key frames.” No fine-tuning, no architectural change.

Plug-and-Play Hi-I Selectors

Different cost–accuracy trade-offs, all drawing from the same decoded candidate pool

LoHi-Uniform

Selects the K Hi-I frames at regular intervals directly from the Lo-V grid. Relying solely on indexing, it adds zero computational overhead and serves as the fundamental baseline of the framework.

LoHi-Anchor

Video encoders anchor GoPs with I-frames, which can align with abrupt scene changes or significant motion. Anchor reads pre-computed I-frame indices without additional pixel decoding: for each uniform position it finds the nearest I-frame and selects the Lo-V grid frame closest to it.

LoHi-SemDiv

Casts Hi-I selection as a quality–similarity DPP over CLIP embeddings of the Lo-V frames: L = diag(q) EE⊤ diag(q). Greedy maximization of log det(LS + I) picks frames that are both query-relevant and mutually diverse, with a (1 − 1/e) guarantee.

Adaptive LoHi. Because the two streams are decoupled, LoHi can first answer from the Lo-V base and trigger the Hi-I stream only when the answer entropy exceeds a threshold. This nearly matches the full pipeline while invoking the high-resolution stream on only one-third of the queries.

Token-pruning methods hit a vision-tower bottleneck because they encode native-resolution frames before pruning. LoHi cuts ViT latency by ~12× (2,486 → 195 ms) and, against the dense low-resolution baseline, raises VideoMME from 64.44% to 66.81% while reducing TTFT from 5,963 to 3,906 ms.

More Findings

Resize beats token pruning

At fixed 25% and 50% token budgets, simply resizing frames matches or outperforms dedicated pruning methods, including MMTok. Resizing downsamples up front and is far cheaper, while pruning pays for native-resolution encoding and breaks spatiotemporal consistency.

Lo-V and Hi-I are complementary

At 5,760 tokens, Lo-V alone (256 frames) reaches 64.44% and Hi-I alone (16 SemDiv images) 63.74%. Splitting the budget into 128 Lo-V frames plus 8 Hi-I images reaches 66.81%.

Robust across K and selectors

All 12 configurations over K ∈ {2, 4, 8} and four training-free selectors reach 65.22–66.81% on VideoMME, above the 128-frame Lo-V-only baseline (63.00%). SemDiv improves over Uniform at every budget.

Generalization. LoHi transfers without retuning to Qwen3-VL-8B, Qwen3.5-4B, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, VideoChat3-4B and VideoLLaMA3-7B. On Qwen3-VL-8B with K = 4 (~25% fewer tokens), LoHi-Anchor reaches 67.70% on VideoMME and LoHi-SemDiv reaches 75.34% on MLVU and 46.80% on LVBench. On Qwen3.5-4B, LoHi-SemDiv reaches 68.15 / 70.42 / 46.68 on VideoMME / MLVU / LVBench.

Sensitivity, or criterion?

Measuring and moving when language models act.

Sixun Dong1, Yebowen Hu1, Ming Yin2, Yiran Chen2, Fei Liu3, Chen Chen1✉ 1University of Central Florida   2Duke University   3Emory University   ✉ corresponding author

Before an agent acts, it has to decide whether to act at all. Benchmarks score that decision with one accuracy, which cannot tell a model that fails to see when an action is warranted from one that sees it and holds back. FlipAct measures the two separately, on an evidence scale that is computed by a rule and is the same for every model.

one

Two observers, one accuracy, opposite mistakes

Start with a case where the stakes are obvious. Two doctors each read the scans of 100 patients who have a tumour and 100 who are healthy. Their accuracy is 65% and 64%. By that number they are the same doctor.

the same 200 cases

Accuracy adds the two kinds of error together. What to do about them depends on which kind you have.

two

Sensitivity and criterion: try it on yourself

Signal-detection theory was worked out in the 1950s for radar operators and for listeners in hearing experiments, to pull those two kinds of error apart. A patch of static flashes on the screen, and half the time it hides a faint blob. You say whether it was there. Two things decide your answers, and they are independent: how well you can see the blob at all, your sensitivity d′, and how sure you want to be before you say yes, your criterion c. Raise the price of a miss and people say yes more freely. Raise the price of a false alarm and they hold back. Their eyesight has not changed.

be the observer
the stakes

The theory draws that as two overlapping curves and one line. Trials without a signal produce some amount of evidence, trials with one produce more, and the observer says yes whenever the evidence lands to the right of their line. How far apart the curves sit is the sensitivity. Where the line sits is the criterion. The picture is the same for a listener, for the two doctors above, and for a language model deciding whether to call a tool.

The same split, wherever an agent decides to act

The doctors' problem is every agent's problem. Calling a tool, asking a question, committing to an answer, executing a change, moving something in the physical world: each is a choice between acting and holding back, each has two kinds of error, and the two rarely cost the same. four

Evidence computed by rule, not written by a model

Comparing criteria across models needs a shared evidence scale with a fixed zero, and authored Yes/No labels do not give one. FlipAct builds it from qualitative physics: the idea that you can say what an action does to a physical state with only a direction and a coarse size, no numbers. A room is slightly, very or dangerously dry or wet. A tool pushes it one way by one, two or three steps. The evidence score ΔU is how much closer the action leaves the state to normal, and the answer is Yes exactly when ΔU > 0.

five

Reading a model: a slope and a crossing point

Because every question carries a score, a model's Yes-rate can be fitted as a curve over that score. The slope a is its sensitivity. The score τ where the curve crosses one half is its criterion. The rule's boundary sits at zero for every model, so a positive τ means the model says Yes less often than the rule warrants.

Sensitivity behaves like a capability

It rises with size inside a family: Qwen3 goes from 0.34 at 4B to 0.58 at 32B. Across models it predicts external d′ on BFCL, QuestBench and When2Call (r = 0.70, 0.69, 0.67).

The criterion is conservative by default

Eighteen of the 21 models with a well-determined criterion sit above zero. They answer Yes less often than the rule warrants, and stay there when the question is rephrased.

It is a per-model setting, not a size effect

Two releases of Mistral-Small-24B are the same size and land on opposite sides of zero, at +2.18 and −1.10.

Models read the sign of the evidence, not its size. Actions that leave the state no better are accepted about as often as helpful ones.

six

Moving the criterion, and only the criterion

If the criterion is a separate setting, it should be possible to move it while sensitivity stays put. Steering does it at inference: a direction read from the model's own activations is added during generation, with the weights fixed. Criterion distillation puts it into the weights: the model is distilled from its own steered copy. Training on the computed labels does the opposite job and raises sensitivity.

It carries to other benchmarks

On six external benchmarks, the criterion moves toward acting on all 23 scored model-benchmark pairs, in every seed, and further than a random direction of the same norm.

Sensitivity is kept

The criterion-free AUC stays within ±0.02 of its starting value for every student on every benchmark where it can be measured.

Accuracy follows the starting point

Where a model starts conservative, balanced accuracy rises, by up to about five points. Where it starts permissive, a shift toward acting only adds false alarms.

A when-to-act benchmark says more when it reports d′ and c beside accuracy.
